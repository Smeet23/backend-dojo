## The problem a breaker solves

When a downstream service fails, each caller keeps trying. Every call waits for a timeout (say 2 seconds), uses a thread or connection while it waits, and then fails anyway. Retries add more of the same. Meanwhile:

- **Users wait** the full timeout for an error that was predictable.
- **Caller resources drain**: threads and connections pile up on a dependency that isn't answering.
- **The failing service never gets a break.** It's hit with full traffic (plus retries) while it's trying to recover, which may be exactly why it can't.

If the last 50 calls to the payment provider failed, the 51st almost certainly will too. A **circuit breaker** acts on that observation: it watches calls to a dependency, and once failures cross a threshold it **stops making the calls** for a while and fails immediately instead. After a pause, it lets a few calls through to check whether the dependency has recovered.

The name comes from electrical circuit breakers, which cut the circuit when current is dangerous, protecting the wiring, until someone resets them. The software version resets itself.

<Callout kind="remember">
Retries assume the failure is brief. Circuit breakers assume it isn't. A resilient client needs both: retries to absorb blips, a breaker to stop hammering something that is properly down.
</Callout>

## The three states

A breaker is a small state machine wrapped around calls to one dependency.

### Closed

Normal operation. Calls pass through to the dependency, and the breaker **records outcomes** (success, failure, slow). When the failure rate over recent calls crosses the threshold, it **trips to open**.

### Open

The breaker **rejects every call immediately** without contacting the dependency, typically by throwing a specific error (Resilience4j's `CallNotPermittedException`) that the caller turns into a fallback. This is fast (microseconds instead of a 2-second timeout), frees the caller's resources, and gives the dependency room to recover.

After a configured **wait duration** (for example 30 seconds), the breaker moves to half-open.

### Half-open

The breaker allows a **small number of trial calls** through (say 3 to 10). Everything else is still rejected.

- If the trial calls succeed (failure rate below threshold), the dependency looks healthy: go back to **closed** and reset the statistics.
- If they fail, it's still broken: go back to **open** and start another wait.

```text
CLOSED     ── failure rate ≥ threshold ──▶  OPEN
OPEN       ── wait duration elapsed ─────▶  HALF-OPEN
HALF-OPEN  ── trial calls succeed ───────▶  CLOSED
HALF-OPEN  ── trial calls fail ──────────▶  OPEN
```

Half-open is what makes the breaker self-healing. Without it, you'd need either a human to reset it, or you'd have to send full traffic to test recovery, which could knock the dependency straight over again.

## Deciding when to trip

### What counts as a failure

Not every error says something about the dependency's health:

| Outcome | Count as failure? |
|---|---|
| Timeout, connection refused/reset | Yes |
| 5xx (500, 502, 503, 504) | Yes |
| 429 Too Many Requests | Usually yes (the dependency is overloaded); some teams prefer to handle it via backoff only |
| 400, 404, 409, 422 | **No**: the dependency is fine, the request was bad or the item doesn't exist |
| Business exceptions ("insufficient funds") | **No** |
| Very slow successes | Often yes, as **slow calls** |

If you count 404s as failures, a burst of lookups for missing items can open the breaker and block lookups for items that exist. Most libraries let you list exceptions to **record** and exceptions to **ignore**.

### Consecutive failures vs failure rate

The simplest breakers count **consecutive failures** ("open after 5 failures in a row"). Easy to understand, but brittle: at high traffic, a few successes interleaved with many failures never trip it, while at low traffic five unlucky failures in an hour can.

Modern breakers use a **failure rate over a sliding window**:

- **Count-based window**: the last N calls (e.g. 100).
- **Time-based window**: calls in the last N seconds (e.g. 60), usually aggregated into per-second buckets.

Trip when the failure rate in the window exceeds a threshold, for example 50%.

### Minimum number of calls

A rate on tiny samples is meaningless: 1 failure out of 2 calls is 50%. A **minimum number of calls** (say 20) must be recorded before the rate is evaluated at all. Pick it based on traffic: a high-volume service can use a large minimum; a low-volume one needs a smaller window or a time-based one.

### Slow calls

A dependency that answers in 8 seconds is effectively down, and it's arguably worse than one that errors, because it holds your resources. Breakers such as Resilience4j can treat calls slower than a **slow-call duration threshold** as bad, and open when the **slow-call rate** crosses its own threshold. This catches degradation that never produces an error.

## Tuning the parameters

A Resilience4j configuration shows the knobs every breaker has, in one form or another:

```yaml
resilience4j:
  circuitbreaker:
    instances:
      payments:
        slidingWindowType: COUNT_BASED
        slidingWindowSize: 50
        minimumNumberOfCalls: 20
        failureRateThreshold: 50          # percent
        slowCallDurationThreshold: 2s
        slowCallRateThreshold: 80         # percent
        waitDurationInOpenState: 30s
        permittedNumberOfCallsInHalfOpenState: 5
        automaticTransitionFromOpenToHalfOpenEnabled: true
        recordExceptions:
          - java.io.IOException
          - java.util.concurrent.TimeoutException
        ignoreExceptions:
          - com.shop.payments.CardDeclinedException
```

| Parameter | Too low | Too high |
|---|---|---|
| Failure rate threshold | Trips on normal noise | Keeps calling a badly broken dependency |
| Window / minimum calls | Decisions on too little data | Slow to react |
| Wait duration in open | Hammers a recovering dependency, flaps open/closed | Stays broken long after the dependency recovered |
| Half-open trial calls | One unlucky call re-opens it | Too much load on a fragile dependency |

Start with library defaults, then tune with real data: the normal error rate of the dependency, its traffic volume, and how long its typical incidents last. Some teams add **exponential backoff on the open wait** (30s, then 60s, then 120s) when the breaker keeps re-opening.

## Fallbacks

When the breaker is open, the caller needs something to return. The breaker only decides *whether* to call; the fallback decides *what the user sees*.

| Dependency | Reasonable fallback |
|---|---|
| Recommendations | Cached or static "popular items" |
| Product reviews | Hide the section |
| Currency rates | Last known rates from a cache, marked as approximate |
| Feature flags | Built-in defaults |
| Payment provider | Fail clearly, or switch to a secondary provider; or accept the order and charge later if the business allows it |
| Fraud check | A business decision: allow low-value orders, hold high-value ones for review |

Some rules for fallbacks:

- **Fallbacks must be cheaper and more reliable than the primary.** A fallback that calls another remote service can fail too, and now you need a fallback for the fallback.
- **Don't hide critical failures.** Silently returning an empty balance or a zero price is worse than an honest error.
- **Make degraded responses visible** in metrics, and where appropriate in the UI ("prices may be out of date").

## One breaker per dependency

A breaker should guard a single **failure domain**. Put one breaker around everything and a failing recommendations service would block payments too, which is the very cascade you're trying to prevent.

Typical granularity:

- **Per downstream service**: the common default.
- **Per endpoint or operation**, when one endpoint can fail independently (a slow report endpoint vs a fast lookup on the same service).
- **Per host or instance**, when instances fail independently. If one of five instances is broken, a service-level breaker sees a 20% failure rate and may not trip, while a per-instance breaker (or **outlier detection** in a load balancer or mesh) removes just the bad instance.
- **Per tenant or region** for multi-region or multi-tenant third-party APIs.

Breaker state is normally **local to each client instance**. With 50 instances, each one trips independently from its own observations. That's usually fine and avoids a shared-state dependency; it just means a few calls per instance hit the failing service before every breaker has opened.

## Combining with retries and bulkheads

The three patterns cover different failure modes and are designed to be stacked:

| Pattern | Protects against | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Timeout** | One call taking forever | Bound each attempt |
| **Retry** | Brief, transient failures | Try again, with backoff and jitter |
| **Bulkhead** | One dependency consuming all resources | Cap concurrent calls per dependency |
| **Circuit breaker** | Sustained failure of a dependency | Stop calling it for a while |

Order matters. The usual stack, from outermost to innermost:

```text
fallback
  └─ retry
       └─ circuit breaker
            └─ bulkhead
                 └─ timeout
                      └─ remote call
```

Why this order:

- **Retry outside the breaker**: each retry attempt goes through the breaker. If the breaker is open, the attempt is rejected instantly, and the retry policy should treat `CallNotPermittedException` as **non-retryable**, so you don't burn the retry schedule hitting a closed door.
- **Breaker outside the bulkhead**: an open breaker rejects calls before they take a bulkhead permit.
- **Timeout innermost**: each attempt is bounded, and timeouts are recorded by the breaker as failures (or slow calls).
- **Fallback outermost**: it catches whatever finally escapes, whether a breaker rejection, a full bulkhead or an exhausted retry.

Should retries count toward the breaker? With the breaker inside the retry, **every attempt** is recorded, which is what you want: three failed attempts are three data points about the dependency's health.

```java
CircuitBreaker cb = CircuitBreaker.of("payments", cbConfig);
Retry retry = Retry.of("payments", RetryConfig.custom()
    .maxAttempts(3)
    .intervalFunction(IntervalFunction.ofExponentialRandomBackoff(200, 2.0))
    .ignoreExceptions(CallNotPermittedException.class)
    .build());

Supplier<Receipt> guarded = Decorators.ofSupplier(() -> payments.charge(order))
    .withCircuitBreaker(cb)
    .withRetry(retry)
    .withFallback(List.of(CallNotPermittedException.class), e -> Receipt.pending(order))
    .decorate();
```

## Libraries and infrastructure

| Tool | Where it runs | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Resilience4j** | In-process (Java/Kotlin) | Circuit breaker, retry, bulkhead, rate limiter, time limiter; integrates with Spring Boot and Micrometer |
| **Hystrix** (Netflix) | In-process (Java) | Popularised the pattern; in maintenance mode since 2018, replaced by Resilience4j |
| **Polly** | In-process (.NET) | Resilience pipelines with breaker, retry, timeout |
| **opossum**, **cockatiel** | In-process (Node.js) | Breaker and policy libraries |
| **sony/gobreaker** | In-process (Go) | Simple state-machine breaker |
| **Envoy / Istio** | Sidecar proxy | Circuit breaking as connection and pending-request limits, plus **outlier detection** that ejects failing hosts |

Note the naming trap: Envoy's "circuit breaking" settings (max connections, max pending requests) behave more like **bulkheads**; its **outlier detection** is closer to the classic per-host breaker.

## Observing breakers

A breaker that opens silently is a hidden outage. Expose:

- **State** per breaker (closed/open/half-open) as a metric, with an alert when a critical one opens.
- **Failure and slow-call rates** that feed the decision.
- **Rejected call counts** (calls short-circuited while open).
- **State transition events** in logs, so incident timelines show when each breaker opened and closed.

A dashboard of breaker states across services is often the fastest way to see where an incident started: the breakers nearest the root cause open first.

## Cheat sheet

- A circuit breaker stops calling a dependency that is clearly failing and fails fast instead.
- Closed: calls flow and outcomes are recorded. Open: calls are rejected instantly. Half-open: a few trial calls decide.
- Trip on failure rate over a sliding window (count- or time-based), not just consecutive failures.
- Require a minimum number of calls before evaluating the rate.
- Count timeouts, 5xx and slow calls as failures; ignore 4xx and business errors.
- Slow-call thresholds catch degradation that never throws an error.
- Wait duration in open gives the dependency time to recover; half-open tests it gently.
- Always pair a breaker with a fallback that is cheaper and more reliable than the primary.
- Use one breaker per dependency (or per endpoint or host), never one for everything.
- Stack as fallback, retry, breaker, bulkhead, timeout; don't retry breaker rejections.
- Resilience4j is the standard JVM library; Hystrix is retired; meshes offer outlier detection.
- Alert on breaker state changes; open breakers point at the source of an incident.
