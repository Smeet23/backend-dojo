## The setting

As soon as data lives on more than one machine, you have **replicas**: copies of the same data on different nodes. You replicate for good reasons. A single node can die and take your data with it; a single node can only serve so many reads; and users on another continent want a copy nearby.

Replicas talk to each other over a network, and networks fail. Messages get delayed, dropped or cut off entirely. The **CAP theorem** (Eric Brewer's conjecture, proved by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002) describes what that forces on you: when the network splits your replicas apart, you can't both keep answering every request **and** guarantee every answer is up to date.

That's the whole theorem in one sentence. The rest of this lesson is about stating it precisely, because most confusion about CAP comes from loose definitions.

## The three properties

### Consistency

In CAP, **consistency** means **linearizability**: the system behaves as if there were a single copy of the data, and every operation takes effect at one instant between its start and its end. Concretely:

- Once a write completes, **every** later read, on any replica, returns that value or a newer one.
- Once any client has read a new value, no client can afterwards read an older one.

This is a much stronger and narrower idea than the **C in ACID**, which means "transactions leave the database satisfying its rules and constraints" (foreign keys, balances not negative). The two share a letter and nothing else. Mixing them up is the most common CAP error.

### Availability

**Availability** in CAP means: **every request received by a non-failing node gets a non-error response.** Not "most requests", not "the cluster is up", but every node that's still running must answer, even if it can't reach anyone else.

That's stricter than the everyday meaning (99.9% uptime). A system that answers from the majority side of a split but returns errors on the minority side is **not** available in the CAP sense, even if 99.99% of users never notice.

### Partition tolerance

A **network partition** is when some nodes can't communicate with others: messages between the groups are lost or delayed indefinitely. **Partition tolerance** means the system keeps operating (in some defined way) despite that, rather than falling into undefined behaviour.

Partitions are not only cut cables. From the point of view of one node, these are all indistinguishable from a partition:

- A switch or router failure, or a misconfigured firewall rule.
- A cross-region link degrading so badly that messages time out.
- A **long garbage-collection pause** or VM freeze: the node is alive but silent for 20 seconds.
- An overloaded node that's too slow to answer heartbeats.

## Partitions are not optional

The popular summary "pick two of three" suggests three equal options: CP, AP or CA. That framing is misleading.

In any system whose nodes communicate over a network, **partitions will happen**. You don't get to choose whether they occur, only how the system behaves when they do. So "CA" (consistent and available, but not partition tolerant) isn't a design you can pick for a distributed system; it describes a system that only works while the network is perfect, and does something undefined otherwise.

A single-node database is "CA" in a trivial sense: there's no network between replicas to partition. The moment you add a replica, you're back to choosing.

<Callout kind="remember">
The honest version of CAP: **during a network partition, a replicated system must choose between consistency and availability.** When there's no partition, you can have both.
</Callout>

## The choice during a partition

Picture a bank balance of 100 on three replicas, A, B and C. The network splits: A and B can talk to each other, C is cut off. A customer asks C to withdraw 60, and at the same moment someone asks A to withdraw 60.

C has two options:

1. **Refuse** (return an error or time out) because it can't confirm with the others that the money is still there. The data stays correct; the request fails. That's choosing **consistency (CP)**.
2. **Accept** the withdrawal locally. The customer gets an answer, but now both sides approved 60 from a balance of 100, and when the partition heals the account is at -20. That's choosing **availability (AP)**.

There's no third option. C can't know what's happening on the other side, and waiting forever is just a slow way of not answering.

A common CP design uses a **majority (quorum)**: the side with a majority of replicas keeps serving reads and writes; the minority side refuses. Since two disjoint groups can't both have a majority, there's never more than one side accepting writes, so there are never conflicting histories.

## CP systems

**CP** systems refuse some requests during a partition rather than risk returning stale data or accepting conflicting writes.

Examples:

- **ZooKeeper, etcd and Consul's key-value store.** Built on consensus (Zab, Raft). A minority partition can't elect a leader or commit writes. That's exactly what you want for leader election, distributed locks and configuration, where two nodes both believing they're the leader is a disaster.
- **HBase** and **Google Bigtable.** Each row range is served by one server at a time; if that server is unreachable, the range is unavailable until it's reassigned.
- **Google Spanner.** Paxos groups with a majority quorum. Google runs it on a private network engineered so partitions are very rare, so it's CP but in practice extremely available.
- **A relational primary with synchronous replication**, or MongoDB with majority write and read concerns.

Use CP where a wrong answer costs more than no answer: account balances, inventory that must not oversell, seat or ticket booking, unique username registration, locks and leases.

## AP systems

**AP** systems keep answering on every reachable node and accept that replicas will temporarily disagree. Once the partition heals, replicas exchange updates and **converge**: that's **eventual consistency**.

Examples:

- **Cassandra** and **Amazon DynamoDB** (with default eventually consistent reads), descendants of Amazon's Dynamo paper, which was designed so the shopping cart could always accept "add to cart".
- **Riak**, **CouchDB** and many multi-leader or leaderless replication setups.
- **DNS.** Records are cached everywhere with TTLs; you always get an answer, possibly an old one.

Accepting writes on both sides means **conflicts** when the partition heals. AP systems need a resolution strategy:

| Strategy | How it works | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Last write wins (LWW) | Keep the value with the highest timestamp | Silently drops a concurrent write; clock skew picks the "wrong" winner |
| Version vectors | Detect that two writes were concurrent and keep both as siblings | The application (or user) must merge them |
| CRDTs | Data types that merge automatically and deterministically: counters, sets, maps | Only certain operations fit; a balance that must not go negative doesn't |
| Application merge | Domain logic, e.g. union of two shopping carts | You write and test it |

Use AP where an answer is better than no answer and temporary disagreement is tolerable: shopping carts, like counts, view counters, social feeds, user presence, product catalogue reads, DNS.

<Callout kind="tip">
Most real products are **not** one letter. An e-commerce site can keep the cart and catalogue AP while checkout and payment are CP. In interviews, make the choice **per feature or per piece of data**, and say why.
</Callout>

## Tunable consistency

Many databases let you choose per operation. Leaderless stores like Cassandra use **quorums**:

- **N**: number of replicas for each key (say 3).
- **W**: how many replicas must acknowledge a write.
- **R**: how many replicas must answer a read.

If **R + W > N**, every read set overlaps every write set in at least one replica, so a read sees the latest acknowledged write (with caveats around concurrent writes and failed partial writes; it's not full linearizability without extra mechanisms).

```text
N = 3
W = 2, R = 2   → R + W = 4 > 3: reads overlap writes, survives 1 node down
W = 1, R = 1   → fastest, but reads may miss the latest write
W = 3, R = 1   → fast reads, but any one node down blocks writes
```

Cassandra exposes this as consistency levels such as `ONE`, `QUORUM`, `LOCAL_QUORUM` and `ALL`. DynamoDB offers eventually consistent reads (cheaper, may be stale) and strongly consistent reads (from the leader replica, twice the read cost, and unavailable if that replica can't be reached).

So "Cassandra is AP" really means "Cassandra lets you choose, and its common configurations favour availability."

## PACELC

CAP only talks about behaviour **during** a partition, which is rare. Most of the time the network is fine, and a different trade-off dominates every single request. **PACELC** (Daniel Abadi) captures both:

> If there is a **P**artition, choose **A**vailability or **C**onsistency; **E**lse, choose **L**atency or **C**onsistency.

Why is there a trade-off without a partition? Because strong consistency requires **coordination**. To guarantee a read is current, you must either read from the leader or contact a quorum, and to acknowledge a write you must wait for other replicas, possibly in other regions. That costs round trips. A system that answers from the nearest replica without asking anyone is fast but can return stale data.

| System | Partition | Else | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cassandra, DynamoDB (default) | A | L | Answer from nearby replicas, converge later |
| Spanner, etcd, ZooKeeper | C | C | Pay consensus latency on every write |
| HBase, Bigtable | C | C | One server owns each range |
| Postgres primary with async replicas | C on the primary | L on replica reads | Replica reads are fast and possibly stale |

<Callout kind="remember">
CAP describes the rare bad day. **PACELC's "else" half is the everyday cost**: every strongly consistent operation pays extra latency for coordination, partition or not.
</Callout>

## Consistency levels

"Consistent" vs "eventually consistent" is too coarse. Between them is a ladder of guarantees, and many products only need a middle rung.

| Level | Guarantee | Typical way to get it |
|---|---|---|
| Linearizable (strong) | Behaves like a single copy; reads see the latest completed write | Read from leader or quorum, consensus |
| Sequential | All clients see operations in the same order, but not necessarily real-time order | Total ordering through a log |
| Causal | If one operation could have influenced another (a reply after the post), everyone sees them in that order | Track dependencies with version vectors |
| Read-your-writes | A user always sees their own updates | Route that user's reads to the leader, or to a replica caught up to their last write |
| Monotonic reads | A user never sees data go backwards in time | Pin a user to one replica, or track the version they've seen |
| Eventual | If writes stop, all replicas converge eventually | Asynchronous replication, anti-entropy |

The middle rows are **session guarantees**: they're about what one user experiences, and they're far cheaper than global linearizability because they only need to track per-user position. A profile edit that the editor can't see on refresh feels like a bug; the same edit taking two seconds to reach other users usually doesn't matter.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Pick any two of three."** Partitions aren't optional, so the real choice is C or A **when partitioned**. Without a partition you can have both.
- **"CA systems exist in distributed databases."** Only if you assume the network never fails, which is assuming the problem away.
- **"The C in CAP is the C in ACID."** CAP's C is linearizability across replicas; ACID's C is about application invariants within a transaction.
- **"A system is either CP or AP."** Most databases are configurable per operation, and a product can make different choices per feature.
- **"AP means no consistency."** AP systems still converge, and can offer session guarantees like read-your-writes.
- **"Available means high uptime."** CAP availability means every non-failing node answers. A CP system with a quorum can still have excellent real-world uptime.
- **"CAP covers latency."** It doesn't. PACELC adds the latency vs consistency trade-off that applies when there's no partition.
- **"Eventual means a long time."** In a healthy cluster replication lag is usually milliseconds. The guarantee is weak; the typical behaviour is good.

## Using CAP in an interview

When a design involves replicated data, walk through it explicitly:

1. **Name the data and what a stale or conflicting value would cost.** A stale like count costs nothing; a double-sold seat costs money and trust.
2. **Pick the behaviour during a partition** for that data: refuse (CP) or answer and reconcile (AP).
3. **Pick the everyday trade-off** (PACELC): is a few milliseconds of coordination acceptable on every request?
4. **Name the mechanism**: quorum reads and writes, leader reads, consensus, LWW, CRDTs, or session routing.
5. **Say how conflicts or stale reads show up to users**, and why that's acceptable.

```text
Ticket booking:
  seat inventory   → CP: majority quorum; minority side rejects bookings
  event listings   → AP: served from nearest replica, stale by seconds is fine
  "my bookings"    → read-your-writes: read from leader for 30 s after a booking
```

## Cheat sheet

- CAP: during a network partition, a replicated system must give up either consistency or availability.
- C means linearizability (acts like one copy), not ACID's C.
- A means every non-failing node returns a non-error response, not just high uptime.
- Partitions include cut links, timeouts, GC pauses and overloaded nodes; you can't opt out of them.
- CP: refuse on the minority side (ZooKeeper, etcd, Spanner, HBase); use for money, inventory, locks.
- AP: answer everywhere and reconcile later (Cassandra, DynamoDB, DNS); use for carts, counters, feeds.
- AP conflicts need LWW, version vectors, CRDTs or application merges.
- Quorums: R + W greater than N makes reads overlap the latest acknowledged write.
- PACELC: else (no partition) choose latency or consistency; strong consistency always costs coordination.
- Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads) fix what users notice at a fraction of the cost.
- Choose per feature and per piece of data, not per company.
